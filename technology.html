Renewable Energy Technologies

At Ecotrunk International Ltd, we believe that no single technology holds all the answers. Kenya's energy transition demands a portfolio approach - selecting the right combination of solar, wind, and hydropower solutions tailored to site-specific conditions, grid infrastructure, community needs, and investment profiles. Below we examine each technology in depth, exploring how they work, where they excel in the Kenyan context, and how our team delivers them from concept to commissioning.

Solar Energy

Harnessing Kenya's Abundant Sunshine

Solar energy technology encompasses two primary pathways for converting sunlight into usable power. Photovoltaic (PV) systems use semiconductor cells to directly transform solar irradiance into electricity - a silent, scalable technology that works from a single rooftop panel to sprawling utility-scale arrays covering hundreds of acres. Concentrating Solar Power (CSP), by contrast, uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver, generating heat that drives a turbine. While CSP remains less common in East Africa due to its capital intensity and water requirements, PV has become the dominant solar technology across Kenya, with installed capacity growing more than fivefold since 2018. Our team at Ecotrunk has designed and supervised PV installations ranging from 10 kW commercial rooftops in Nairobi to multi-megawatt ground-mounted farms in the Rift Valley.

Kenya possesses some of the finest solar resources on the planet. The Great Rift Valley region, stretching from Lake Turkana in the north to Lake Magadi in the south, receives insolation levels of 5.5 to 6.5 kWh per square metre per day - among the highest in the world. This solar dividend creates compelling economics: at current module prices below USD 0.15 per watt, unsubsidised solar electricity in Kenya frequently undercuts diesel generation by 60 to 80 percent. Commercial and industrial users in Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu are increasingly turning to rooftop solar not as an environmental gesture but as a hard-nosed financial decision, with simple payback periods of three to five years. On the utility side, large-scale solar farms are beginning to feed power into the national grid, supported by Kenya's feed-in tariff policy and the growing appetite of international climate financiers.

The integration of battery energy storage systems is transforming what solar can deliver. Early solar installations in Kenya were constrained by the sun's intermittency - power was only available during daylight hours, and grid instability meant that solar systems often had to shut down during frequency excursions. Modern lithium-ion and flow battery solutions have changed this calculus. By pairing PV arrays with battery storage, our projects can now deliver firm, dispatchable power that operates through the evening peak, stabilises voltage fluctuations, and provides backup during grid outages. The cost of battery storage has fallen by more than 80 percent over the past decade, and Ecotrunk is actively deploying hybrid solar-plus-storage systems for commercial clients who demand 24-hour energy availability. We expect this trend to accelerate as Kenya's grid evolves toward higher renewable penetration and as battery costs continue their downward trajectory.

  • Commercial & Industrial Rooftop PV - Reducing operational energy costs for businesses, factories, and office complexes across Kenya's urban centres
  • Utility-Scale Solar Farms - Developing ground-mounted PV installations from 1 MW to 50 MW connected to the national grid via power purchase agreements
  • Mini-Grid & Off-Grid Systems - Deploying decentralised solar solutions for rural communities, schools, and health facilities not reached by grid extension
  • Solar-Plus-Storage Hybrids - Integrating lithium-ion and advanced lead-carbon battery banks to provide firm capacity and grid support services
  • Solar Water Pumping & Agricultural Applications - Powering irrigation schemes, livestock watering, and crop drying for Kenya's agricultural sector

Wind Energy

Capturing the Power of Kenya's Wind Corridors

Wind energy technology converts the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity using turbine generators mounted on tall towers. The fundamental principle is elegantly simple: wind passing over the turbine blades creates lift, causing the rotor to spin a shaft connected to a generator. Modern onshore turbines typically range from 1.5 MW to 6 MW in rated capacity, with hub heights of 80 to 140 metres and rotor diameters exceeding 150 metres. Offshore wind turbines, while less relevant to Kenya's current market, are built to withstand marine environments and can reach even larger capacities. The technological trajectory has been relentless - taller towers access stronger, more consistent wind speeds, while advanced blade aerodynamics, direct-drive permanent magnet generators, and sophisticated power electronics have pushed capacity factors from 25 percent two decades ago to over 45 percent in prime locations today.

Kenya is blessed with some of Africa's most promising wind resources. The Turkana Corridor in northern Kenya experiences powerful channeled winds that average 11 to 13 metres per second during the peak season - among the strongest measured anywhere on the continent. The Lake Turkana Wind Power project, with 365 turbines producing 310 MW, stands as Africa's largest wind farm and a testament to what is possible when technology meets political will. The Ngong Hills, overlooking Nairobi, have hosted wind turbines since the 1990s and continue to demonstrate the viability of ridge-top installations near major load centres. Our wind resource assessment work at Ecotrunk involves deploying meteorological masts and SODAR units to collect at least twelve months of site-specific data before committing to turbine selection and layout design. We have conducted pre-feasibility studies for wind projects in Marsabit, Laikipia, Kajiado, and along the coastal escarpments - each location offering a distinct wind profile that demands careful matching of turbine type to site conditions.

Wind power integration into Kenya's grid presents both technical challenges and substantial opportunities. The national grid, operated by Kenya Power and Kenya Electricity Transmission Company, was originally designed around predictable hydropower and geothermal baseload. Wind's inherent variability - ramping up and down with changing weather patterns - requires sophisticated forecasting, dispatch scheduling, and sometimes curtailment protocols. Ecotrunk's engineering teams work closely with system operators to develop wind farm control systems that comply with Kenya's grid code, providing reactive power capability, frequency response, and fault ride-through. Beyond the technical, wind energy delivers profound benefits to host communities. Project revenues fund local infrastructure, education scholarships, and healthcare programmes through community development agreements. In Turkana, the wind farm has brought all-weather roads, reliable mobile phone coverage, and employment opportunities to one of Kenya's most marginalised regions. We see community engagement not as a regulatory box to tick but as a foundation for project success and long-term social license.

  • Onshore Wind Farm Development - End-to-end project delivery from wind resource assessment and turbine selection to construction and grid connection
  • Repowering & Retrofit Solutions - Upgrading existing wind farms with modern, higher-capacity turbines and improved control systems
  • Hybrid Wind-Solar Systems - Combining complementary wind and solar profiles to smooth overall generation and improve capacity factors
  • Community Wind Projects - Developing smaller-scale turbines for rural electrification, water pumping, and productive uses in off-grid areas
  • Wind Measurement & Feasibility Studies - Deploying meteorological monitoring campaigns, data analysis, and financial modelling to de-risk investment decisions

Hydropower

Leveraging Kenya's Rivers for Clean Baseload Power

Hydropower captures the energy of water moving from higher to lower elevations, converting gravitational potential energy into electricity through turbines coupled to generators. The technology encompasses a broad spectrum of project types. Large dam projects with reservoirs store vast quantities of water, providing multi-season storage that can dispatch power on demand - these are the workhorses of many national grids, capable of supplying hundreds of megawatts of firm capacity. Run-of-river projects, by contrast, divert a portion of a river's flow through a turbine without significant impoundment, offering lower environmental impact but greater sensitivity to seasonal flow variations. In-stream and small-scale hydro installations extend the technology to smaller rivers and community-level applications. Each configuration carries distinct trade-offs among capacity, cost, environmental footprint, and operational flexibility. At Ecotrunk, our hydropower practice spans the full range, from pre-feasibility desk studies to detailed design and construction supervision.

Kenya's hydropower potential is concentrated along the Tana River basin, the Athi River system, and the numerous rivers draining the Aberdare Range and Mount Kenya. The Seven Forks cascade on the Tana River - comprising Masinga, Kamburu, Gitaru, Kindaruma, and Kiambere - has been the backbone of Kenya's electricity system since the 1970s, with a combined installed capacity exceeding 500 MW. More recently, run-of-river projects on the Sondu Miriu and Turkwel rivers have added capacity while demonstrating lower reservoir footprints. Our involvement in Kenya's hydro sector includes hydrological assessments for new run-of-river schemes in Nandi and Murang'a counties, feasibility studies for small hydro projects capable of powering tea factories and agro-processing hubs, and technical audits of existing plants to identify performance improvement opportunities. The strategic value of hydropower lies in its dispatchability - unlike solar and wind, hydro can be called upon when needed, providing crucial grid stabilisation services as Kenya adds more variable renewable generation.

Environmental and climate considerations are central to responsible hydropower development. Large reservoirs can alter river ecology, displace communities, and emit methane from decomposing vegetation - concerns that must be rigorously addressed through environmental and social impact assessments. Run-of-river and small-scale hydro projects typically carry a lighter environmental footprint, making them attractive options for rural electrification in Kenya's highland regions where perennial rivers flow with reliable gradients. Ecotrunk advocates for climate-resilient hydropower design: incorporating sediment management strategies to extend reservoir life, designing spillways for extreme flood events under changing rainfall patterns, and maintaining environmental flow releases to support downstream ecosystems. For remote communities beyond the grid, small hydro plants of 10 kW to 500 kW can provide reliable 24-hour electricity that transforms livelihoods - powering schools, clinics, small enterprises, and homes. We have developed a toolkit for community-scale hydro that combines technical standardisation with local capacity building, ensuring that projects remain operable and maintainable long after commissioning.

  • Run-of-River Hydro Schemes - Environmentally-sensitive projects that harness river flows without large reservoirs, ideal for Kenya's highland catchments
  • Reservoir Hydro & Multi-Purpose Dams - Large-scale projects combining electricity generation with irrigation, water supply, and flood management benefits
  • Small & Mini Hydro for Rural Electrification - Community-scale plants from 10 kW to 1 MW serving off-grid populations and productive enterprises
  • Hydro Rehabilitation & Modernisation - Upgrading ageing turbines, control systems, and civil infrastructure to improve efficiency, capacity, and safety
  • Climate Resilience & Sediment Management - Designing for extreme hydrology, sediment bypass, and environmental flow compliance in a changing climate

Why Technology Selection Matters

Choosing the wrong renewable energy technology for a given site can mean years of underperformance, unexpected costs, and missed opportunities. A solar farm sited in a cloudy microclimate, a wind turbine placed in turbulent terrain, or a hydro plant designed around outdated hydrology data - each represents a failure of the feasibility process that Ecotrunk is specifically structured to prevent.

Our approach combines rigorous technical analysis with deep local knowledge. We invest in site-specific data collection - meteorological monitoring, hydrological surveys, geotechnical investigations, and grid impact studies - before recommending a technology pathway. We model multiple scenarios, test sensitivities against fuel price volatility and policy changes, and engage communities early to understand land-use priorities and social dynamics. The result is a technology selection that is defensible, financeable, and built to deliver returns over decades of operation.
