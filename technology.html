Solar Energy

Harnessing Kenya's Abundant Sunshine

Solar energy technology encompasses two primary pathways for converting sunlight into usable power. Photovoltaic (PV) systems use semiconductor cells to directly transform solar irradiance into electricity - a silent, scalable technology that works from a single rooftop panel to sprawling utility-scale arrays covering hundreds of acres. Concentrating Solar Power (CSP), by contrast, uses mirrors or lenses to focus sunlight onto a receiver, generating heat that drives a turbine. While CSP remains less common in East Africa due to its capital intensity and water requirements, PV has become the dominant solar technology across Kenya, with installed capacity growing more than fivefold since 2018.

Kenya possesses some of the finest solar resources on the planet. The Great Rift Valley region, stretching from Lake Turkana in the north to Lake Magadi in the south, receives insolation levels of 5.5 to 6.5 kWh per square metre per day - among the highest recorded anywhere in the world. This solar dividend creates compelling economics: at current module prices below USD 0.15 per watt, unsubsidised solar electricity in Kenya frequently undercuts diesel generation by 60 to 80 percent. Commercial and industrial users in Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu are increasingly turning to rooftop solar not as an environmental gesture but as a hard-nosed financial decision, with simple payback periods of three to five years. On the utility side, large-scale solar farms are beginning to feed power into the national grid, supported by Kenya's feed-in tariff policy and the growing appetite of international climate financiers.

Solar PV technology has undergone dramatic cost reductions over the past decade. Module prices have fallen by over 90 percent since 2010, driven by manufacturing scale in China, improvements in cell efficiency, and advances in manufacturing processes. Modern monocrystalline panels achieve efficiency ratings of 21 to 23 percent, up from 15 percent a decade ago, meaning more electricity is generated per square metre of panel area. Bifacial modules, which capture light on both sides, are becoming standard in utility-scale installations, boosting energy yield by 5 to 15 percent. Inverter technology has similarly advanced, with string inverters giving way to microinverters and power optimisers that maximise harvest from each individual panel, particularly important in installations where partial shading is unavoidable.

The integration of battery energy storage systems is transforming what solar can deliver. Early solar installations in Kenya were constrained by the sun's intermittency - power was only available during daylight hours, and grid instability meant that solar systems often had to shut down during frequency excursions. Modern lithium-ion and flow battery solutions have changed this calculus. The cost of battery storage has fallen by more than 80 percent over the past decade, from over USD 1,000 per kWh in 2015 to under USD 150 per kWh today. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry has emerged as the preferred choice for stationary storage due to its safety profile, long cycle life exceeding 6,000 cycles, and lower environmental impact. By pairing PV arrays with battery storage, our projects at Ecotrunk now deliver firm, dispatchable power that operates through the evening peak, stabilises voltage fluctuations, and provides backup during grid outages. We are actively deploying hybrid solar-plus-storage systems for commercial clients who demand 24-hour energy availability.

Beyond grid-connected applications, solar is expanding access to electricity for the estimated 25 percent of Kenyans who remain off-grid. Solar mini-grids, combining PV arrays with battery storage and smart metering, offer a faster and more cost-effective path to electrification than conventional grid extension. Advances in remote monitoring and mobile payment platforms have made these systems commercially sustainable at smaller scales than ever before. Agricultural solar applications are also growing rapidly: solar water pumping for irrigation is replacing expensive and unreliable diesel pumps across arid and semi-arid regions, reducing operating costs by up to 90 percent while enabling climate-resilient farming practices.

  • Commercial & Industrial Rooftop PV - Reducing operational energy costs for businesses, factories, and office complexes across Kenya's urban centres with minimal land use and fast installation timelines
  • Utility-Scale Solar Farms - Ground-mounted PV installations from 1 MW to 50 MW connected to the national grid via power purchase agreements, with full EPC and financing support
  • Mini-Grid & Off-Grid Systems - Decentralised solar solutions for rural communities, schools, and health facilities beyond grid reach, incorporating smart metering and mobile payments

  • Solar Water Pumping & Agriculture - Powering irrigation schemes, livestock watering, and agro-processing with clean, reliable solar energy replacing diesel pumps

Wind Energy

Capturing the Power of Kenya's Wind Corridors

Wind energy technology converts the kinetic energy of moving air into electricity using turbine generators mounted on tall towers. The fundamental principle is elegantly simple: wind passing over the turbine blades creates aerodynamic lift, causing the rotor to spin a shaft connected to a generator. Modern onshore turbines typically range from 1.5 MW to 6 MW in rated capacity, with hub heights of 80 to 140 metres and rotor diameters exceeding 150 metres. The technological trajectory over the past two decades has been relentless - taller towers access stronger, more consistent wind speeds at higher elevations, while advanced blade aerodynamics with serrated trailing edges and winglet designs have improved energy capture by 20 to 30 percent compared to early-generation turbines.

Kenya is blessed with some of Africa's most promising wind resources, thanks to unique topographical and meteorological conditions. The Turkana Corridor in northern Kenya experiences powerful channeled winds that average 11 to 13 metres per second during the peak season from June to September - among the strongest wind speeds measured anywhere on the continent. This phenomenon is created by the funneling effect of the Turkana Channel, where the Ethiopian and Kenyan highlands force air through a narrow gap, dramatically accelerating wind speeds. The Lake Turkana Wind Power project, with 365 turbines producing 310 MW, stands as Africa's largest wind farm and a landmark achievement in Kenyan renewable energy infrastructure, supplying approximately 15 percent of the country's installed generation capacity. The Ngong Hills, overlooking Nairobi, have hosted wind turbines since the 1990s and continue to demonstrate the viability of ridge-top installations near major load centres, with the 25.5 MW Ngong Wind Farm feeding directly into the Nairobi grid.

The economics of wind power have improved dramatically as turbine technology has matured. The global levelised cost of onshore wind energy has fallen by over 60 percent since 2010, making it one of the most cost-competitive sources of new electricity generation in regions with good wind resources. Modern turbines incorporate sophisticated control systems that optimise blade pitch and yaw in real time, responding to changing wind conditions to maximise energy capture while minimising structural loads. Direct-drive permanent magnet generators have eliminated the gearbox - historically the most failure-prone component of wind turbines - improving reliability and reducing maintenance costs. At Ecotrunk, our wind resource assessment work involves deploying meteorological masts equipped with anemometers at multiple heights, as well as SODAR (Sonic Detection and Ranging) units that measure wind speed and direction profiles up to 200 metres. We collect a minimum of twelve months of site-specific data before committing to turbine selection and layout design, ensuring that every turbine is placed for optimal energy capture given the site's specific wind rose and turbulence characteristics.

Wind power integration into Kenya's grid presents both technical challenges and substantial opportunities. The national grid, operated by Kenya Power and the Kenya Electricity Transmission Company (KETRACO), was originally designed around predictable hydropower and geothermal baseload generation. Wind's inherent variability - ramping up and down with changing weather patterns - requires sophisticated forecasting systems, dynamic dispatch scheduling, and well-designed curtailment protocols to maintain grid stability. Wind farms must comply with Kenya's Grid Code requirements for reactive power capability, frequency response, and fault ride-through - all of which our engineering teams incorporate into project design. Beyond the technical dimensions, wind energy delivers profound economic and social benefits to host communities. The Lake Turkana project has brought all-weather roads, reliable mobile phone coverage, and employment opportunities to one of Kenya's most historically marginalised regions. Community development agreements funded through project revenues support local schools, healthcare facilities, and infrastructure. At Ecotrunk, we view community engagement not as a regulatory requirement to satisfy but as a foundation for project success and long-term social license to operate.

  • Onshore Wind Farm Development - End-to-end project delivery from wind resource assessment and turbine selection to construction, commissioning, and grid connection
  • Repowering & Retrofit Solutions - Upgrading existing wind farms with modern, higher-capacity turbines and advanced control systems to improve energy capture and extend operational life
  • Hybrid Wind-Solar Systems - Combining complementary wind and solar generation profiles to smooth overall output, improve capacity factors, and share grid connection infrastructure
  • Community Wind Projects - Developing smaller-scale single turbine or small cluster installations for rural electrification, water pumping, and productive uses in off-grid areas
  • Wind Measurement & Feasibility Studies - Comprehensive meteorological monitoring campaigns, data analysis, energy yield assessment, and financial modelling to de-risk investment decisions

Hydropower

Leveraging Kenya's Rivers for Clean Baseload Power

Hydropower captures the energy of water moving from higher to lower elevations, converting gravitational potential energy into electricity through turbines coupled to generators. The technology encompasses a broad spectrum of project types, each suited to different hydrological and topographical conditions. Large dam projects with reservoirs store vast quantities of water, providing multi-season storage that can dispatch power on demand - these are the workhorses of many national grids, capable of supplying hundreds of megawatts of firm, dispatchable capacity. Run-of-river projects, by contrast, divert a portion of a river's flow through a turbine without significant impoundment, offering lower environmental impact and reduced upfront cost but greater sensitivity to seasonal flow variations. In-stream and small-scale hydro installations extend the technology to smaller rivers and community-level applications, with typical capacities ranging from 10 kW to 1 MW. At Ecotrunk, our hydropower practice spans the full spectrum, from pre-feasibility desk studies using satellite-derived hydrological data to detailed design of intake structures, penstocks, turbine halls, and tailrace systems.

Kenya's hydropower potential is concentrated along the Tana River basin, the Athi River system, and the numerous rivers draining the Aberdare Range and Mount Kenya. The Seven Forks cascade on the Tana River - comprising Masinga, Kamburu, Gitaru, Kindaruma, and Kiambere - has been the backbone of Kenya's electricity system since the 1970s, with a combined installed capacity exceeding 500 MW. The cascade demonstrates the strategic value of multi-reservoir systems: water released from upper reservoirs can generate electricity at multiple downstream plants before being used for irrigation or flowing to the Indian Ocean. More recently, run-of-river projects on the Sondu Miriu and Turkwel rivers have added over 80 MW of capacity while demonstrating lower reservoir footprints and reduced environmental displacement. Our involvement in Kenya's hydro sector includes hydrological assessments for new run-of-river schemes in Nandi and Murang'a counties, feasibility studies for small hydro projects capable of powering tea factories and agro-processing hubs, and technical audits of existing plants to identify performance improvement opportunities through turbine refurbishment and control system modernisation.

Different turbine technologies are suited to different head and flow conditions. Pelton turbines are ideal for high-head, low-flow applications common in Kenya's mountainous catchments, achieving efficiencies above 90 percent. Francis turbines, the most widely used type worldwide, operate efficiently across a broad range of head and flow conditions and are the standard choice for medium-to-large hydro projects. Kaplan turbines, with their adjustable blades, excel in low-head, high-flow applications such as run-of-river schemes on larger rivers. Each turbine type requires careful matching to site-specific conditions - a Pelton turbine operating outside its design flow range can lose 20 to 30 percent efficiency, dramatically affecting project economics. Our engineering team uses computational fluid dynamics (CFD) modelling to optimise turbine selection, intake design, and penstock alignment for each project, maximising energy capture while minimising civil works costs.

Environmental and climate considerations are central to responsible hydropower development. Large reservoirs can alter river ecology, displace communities, trap sediment that would otherwise nourish downstream floodplains, and emit methane from decomposing vegetation - concerns that must be rigorously addressed through comprehensive environmental and social impact assessments (ESIAs). Run-of-river and small-scale hydro projects typically carry a lighter environmental footprint, making them attractive options for rural electrification in Kenya's highland regions where perennial rivers flow with reliable gradients. Ecotrunk advocates for climate-resilient hydropower design: incorporating sediment management strategies such as flushing sluices and desilting basins to extend reservoir life; designing spillways for extreme flood events projected under climate change scenarios; and maintaining environmental flow releases to support downstream aquatic ecosystems and livelihoods. For remote communities beyond the national grid, small hydro plants of 10 kW to 500 kW can provide reliable 24-hour electricity that transforms livelihoods - powering schools for evening classes, running grain mills and coffee pulpers, operating cold storage for vaccines, and lighting small enterprises. We have developed a standardised toolkit for community-scale hydro that combines repeatable design templates with local capacity building, ensuring that projects remain operable and maintainable by local technicians long after commissioning.

  • Run-of-River Hydro Schemes - Environmentally-sensitive projects that harness river flows without large reservoirs, ideal for Kenya's highland catchments with perennial rivers
  • Reservoir Hydro & Multi-Purpose Dams - Large-scale projects combining electricity generation with irrigation, water supply, flood management, and recreation benefits
  • Small & Mini Hydro for Rural Electrification - Community-scale plants from 10 kW to 1 MW serving off-grid populations, tea factories, schools, and health centres
  • Hydro Rehabilitation & Modernisation - Upgrading ageing turbines, governors, control systems, and civil infrastructure to improve efficiency, capacity, and operational safety
  • Climate Resilience & Sediment Management - Designing for extreme hydrological events, sediment bypass and desilting, and environmental flow compliance under changing climate conditions
