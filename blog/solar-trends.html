Kenya's Solar Revolution: Five Trends Shaping 2026

By Ecotrunk Research Team | 10 min read

Kenya's solar energy market has reached an inflection point. With module prices consistently below USD 0.15 per watt and commercial electricity tariffs among the highest in Africa at approximately KES 15–20 per kWh, the economic case for solar has never been more compelling. Installed solar capacity in Kenya has grown from roughly 100 MW in 2019 to over 500 MW by early 2026, driven by a combination of falling hardware costs, innovative financing models, and supportive government policy. Here are five trends we believe will define the market in 2026 and beyond.

1. Commercial and Industrial Rooftop Solar Dominates

Businesses across Nairobi, Mombasa, Kisumu, and Nakuru are increasingly turning to rooftop solar not as an environmental gesture but as a straightforward financial imperative. With system payback periods of three to five years and module lifespans exceeding 25 years, the return on investment is unambiguous. We are seeing particularly strong uptake in three sectors where energy costs represent a significant share of operating expenses.

In manufacturing, factories in industrial zones such as Athi River, Ruaraka, and Mombasa's Changamwe are installing rooftop arrays ranging from 100 kW to 2 MW to hedge against unreliable grid supply and rising tariffs. A typical cement or steel fabrication facility can reduce its grid consumption by 30–40 percent through daytime solar generation alone, with some facilities achieving payback in under three years where diesel backup costs are displaced.

The hospitality sector — hotels, lodges, and resorts from the coast to the Maasai Mara — has embraced solar both for cost savings and for brand positioning in the eco-tourism market. Lodges that were previously reliant on diesel generators for 24-hour power are now running hybrid solar-battery systems that eliminate fuel costs, reduce noise pollution, and appeal to environmentally conscious guests. The 250-room Sarova Whitesands Beach Resort in Mombasa, for instance, operates a 650 kW rooftop installation that covers roughly 30 percent of its energy demand.

Tea and coffee processors in the highlands of Kericho, Nandi, and Murang'a are deploying solar to power drying, grading, and packaging operations. With tea factories operating primarily during daylight hours, solar PV aligns naturally with their load profile, offering internal rates of return exceeding 20 percent on typical installations. The Kenya Tea Development Agency has reported that over 20 of its 68 factories have installed or are planning solar PV systems, with individual installations ranging from 150 kW to 500 kW.

2. Solar-Plus-Storage Becomes Mainstream

The dramatic decline in lithium-ion battery storage costs has transformed what solar can deliver for Kenyan businesses and institutions. Battery pack prices have fallen from over USD 300 per kWh in 2018 to approximately USD 90 per kWh for LFP (lithium iron phosphate) systems in 2026, a decline of more than 70 percent. This cost trajectory has made solar-plus-storage the default recommendation for any facility requiring reliable 24-hour power.

LFP chemistry has emerged as the technology of choice for commercial and industrial storage applications, offering superior thermal stability, longer cycle life (4,000–6,000 cycles), and lower total cost of ownership compared to older NMC chemistries. Modern systems enable businesses to store excess daytime generation and discharge during the evening peak window between 6 PM and 10 PM, when grid tariffs are highest and the risk of load-shedding is greatest. For a typical Nairobi office building, a solar-plus-storage system sized at 100 kW solar with 200 kWh of battery storage can achieve grid independence for 70–80 percent of annual consumption.

C&I electricity tariffs in Kenya are structured with both energy charges (KES 7–12 per kWh) and demand charges (KES 400–800 per kVA per month). Solar-plus-storage systems reduce both: energy charges through solar generation and demand charges through battery peak shaving. The combined savings yield project returns of 15–25 percent IRR for well-designed systems in the 50 kW to 1 MW range. For facilities such as hospitals, cold storage warehouses, and data centres that cannot tolerate power interruptions, the value of uninterrupted power supply from solar-plus-storage often exceeds the energy cost savings alone.

3. Utility-Scale Solar Attracts International Capital

Kenya's pipeline of utility-scale solar projects has swelled to over 1 GW, driven by the government's commitment to achieving 100 percent renewable electricity by 2030. The feed-in tariff policy, combined with the growing appetite of international climate financiers, is catalysing a wave of large-scale solar development across the Rift Valley and Eastern regions, where insolation levels range from 5.5 to 6.5 kWh per square metre per day — among the best in the world.

Kenya has adopted an REIPPPP-style auction framework modelled on South Africa's successful programme, with competitive bidding driving PPA prices downward. The first round of renewable energy auctions in late 2025 attracted bids as low as USD 0.04 per kWh, a price point that rivals hydro and geothermal generation. Development finance institutions including the World Bank's IFC, the African Development Bank, and the European Investment Bank have committed substantial climate finance facilities to Kenyan solar projects, with blended finance instruments — concessional loans combined with commercial debt — de-risking early-stage development and lowering the cost of capital.

Projects such as the 50 MW Alten Solar Farm in Malindi, the 40 MW Kesses plant in Uasin Gishu, and the proposed 200 MW Eldosol complex near Eldoret demonstrate the scale of capital flowing into the sector. Kenya's partial risk guarantee framework, provided through the World Bank's IDA, has been instrumental in attracting independent power producers by backstopping Kenya Power's payment obligations under PPAs. The combination of world-class solar resources, improving regulatory frameworks, and patient international capital positions Kenya as one of Africa's most attractive utility-scale solar markets.

4. Mini-Grids Bridge the Access Gap

Despite significant progress under the Last Mile Connectivity Programme, an estimated 25 percent of Kenyans — roughly 12 million people — remain without access to grid electricity. These households and businesses are predominantly in rural, low-population-density areas where traditional grid extension is prohibitively expensive. Extending a medium-voltage line across 10 kilometres of remote terrain can cost KES 8–15 million, serving perhaps 200–300 households. Solar mini-grids offer a faster, more cost-effective path to electrification, with costs per connection of KES 40,000–60,000 compared to KES 100,000–200,000 for grid extension in similar terrain.

The mini-grid sector has been transformed by innovations in smart metering, remote monitoring via IoT platforms, and mobile money integration. Companies such as M-KOPA, Powergen, and Virunga Power have pioneered PAYG (pay-as-you-go) models that enable households to pay for electricity in small daily instalments via M-Pesa, eliminating upfront connection fees that previously excluded low-income families. Modern mini-grids combine solar PV with battery storage and, increasingly, a small diesel or biogas generator for resilience, delivering reliable 24-hour power to communities of 50 to 5,000 households.

The Rural Electrification and Renewable Energy Corporation (REREC) has incorporated mini-grids into its formal electrification planning, with over 150 solar mini-grids installed or under development across 20 counties. Kenya's Mini-Grid Policy, released in 2022, provides a clear licensing framework, standardised tariff-setting methodology, and a pathway for mini-grids to interconnect with the national grid when it arrives. This regulatory clarity has unlocked commercial investment, with private developers now accounting for over 60 percent of new mini-grid capacity.

5. Agricultural Solar Applications Expand

Agriculture accounts for roughly 30 percent of Kenya's GDP and employs the majority of the workforce, yet the sector remains severely under-electrified. Solar technology is beginning to change this, with three applications driving the fastest growth in agri-solar adoption across the country.

Solar water pumping for irrigation is transforming farming in arid and semi-arid regions such as Kitui, Makueni, and Laikipia. With diesel pump operating costs of KES 25,000–40,000 per month for a typical smallholder operating a 5 HP pump for four hours daily, a solar pump pays for itself within 12–18 months and then delivers essentially free water for the remainder of its 10-year lifespan. The Kenya Climate-Smart Agriculture Project and various county governments are offering subsidies and financing schemes that have accelerated adoption to an estimated 10,000 solar pumps installed annually. Solar pumping also enables drip irrigation and other water-efficient techniques that improve crop yields by 50–200 percent.

Solar cold storage is addressing the staggering post-harvest loss rates that plague Kenya's horticulture and dairy sectors, where 30–40 percent of fresh produce is lost before reaching market. Off-grid solar walk-in cold rooms, powered by DC compressors and LFP batteries, enable farmers and cooperatives to store tomatoes, mangoes, milk, and leafy greens for days or weeks, commanding higher prices during off-peak market windows. Companies such as SolarCool and InspiraFarms have deployed over 200 solar cold rooms across Kenya, with individual units storing 5–10 tonnes of produce.

Solar crop drying offers a controlled, energy-efficient alternative to open-air sun drying for coffee, tea, maize, and horticultural products. Hybrid solar-biomass dryers reduce drying time by 30–50 percent, improve product quality by eliminating contamination from dust and pests, and enable farmers to achieve premium grades for export markets. As climate adaptation funding increasingly targets agricultural resilience, we expect investment in solar-powered cold chains and drying infrastructure to accelerate substantially through 2027 and beyond.

Conclusion: A Sector at Critical Mass

The five trends outlined above are not occurring in isolation. Falling battery costs make C&I solar more valuable by enabling evening peak shaving. Utility-scale auctions create demonstration effects that build confidence for mini-grid financiers. Agricultural solar applications open entirely new demand segments that attract further private capital into the broader solar ecosystem. Kenya's solar revolution is entering a virtuous cycle in which each success builds the foundation for the next. For developers, the imperative is to design systems that capture the full value stack — energy, capacity, reliability, and decarbonisation. For financiers, the opportunity is to deploy innovative instruments — green bonds, results-based financing, and securitised PAYG receivables — that match the sector's growing maturity. For policymakers, the task is clear: remove remaining barriers to interconnection, streamline permitting, and ensure that Kenya's extraordinary solar resource delivers universal energy access and sustainable prosperity for all.
