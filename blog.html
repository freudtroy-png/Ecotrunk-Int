Kenya's Solar Revolution: Five Trends Shaping 2026

By Ecotrunk Research Team | 6 min read

Kenya's solar energy market has reached an inflection point. With module prices below USD 0.15 per watt and commercial electricity tariffs among the highest in Africa, the economics of solar have never been more compelling. Here are five trends we believe will define the market in 2026.

1. Commercial and Industrial Rooftop Solar Dominates

Businesses in Nairobi, Mombasa, and Kisumu are increasingly turning to rooftop solar not as an environmental gesture but as a financial imperative. With payback periods of three to five years and system lifespans exceeding 25 years, the return on investment is clear. We are seeing particular growth in the manufacturing, hospitality, and agro-processing sectors where energy costs represent a significant portion of operating expenses.

2. Solar-Plus-Storage Becomes Mainstream

The dramatic decline in battery storage costs has transformed what solar can deliver. Modern lithium-ion systems enable businesses to store excess daytime generation for use during evening peaks, reducing grid dependence by up to 80 percent. For facilities that require 24-hour power reliability, solar-plus-storage is now the default recommendation.

3. Utility-Scale Solar Attracts International Capital

Kenya's feed-in tariff policy and the growing appetite of international climate financiers are driving a pipeline of large-scale solar projects. Several multi-megawatt solar farms are in development across the Rift Valley, taking advantage of the region's exceptional insolation levels of 5.5 to 6.5 kWh per square metre per day.

4. Mini-Grids Bridge the Access Gap

For the estimated 25 percent of Kenyans without grid access, solar mini-grids offer a faster, more cost-effective path to electrification than grid extension. Advances in smart metering, remote monitoring, and mobile payment integration are making mini-grids commercially sustainable at smaller scales than ever before.

5. Agricultural Solar Applications Expand

Solar water pumping for irrigation is transforming farming in arid and semi-arid regions. With diesel pump operating costs eliminated and maintenance requirements minimal, farmers are adopting solar pumping at accelerating rates. We expect this trend to accelerate as climate adaptation funding targets agricultural resilience.

Lessons from Lake Turkana: Building Africa's Largest Wind Farm

By Ecotrunk Engineering Team | 8 min read

The Lake Turkana Wind Power project stands as Africa's largest wind farm, with 365 turbines producing 310 MW of clean electricity. Located in one of Kenya's most remote and challenging environments, the project offers invaluable lessons for renewable energy development across the continent.

Engineering in Extreme Conditions

The Turkana Corridor experiences some of the strongest and most consistent winds on the continent, with peak speeds of 11 to 13 metres per second. However, the remote location demanded innovative logistics: all 365 turbines, each weighing hundreds of tonnes, were transported over 1,200 kilometres from Mombasa port through rough terrain. This required upgrading roads, building temporary bridges, and coordinating convoys across multiple counties. The lesson for future projects is clear: logistics planning must begin during feasibility, not after financial close.

Community Engagement as a Foundation

The project brought all-weather roads, reliable mobile phone coverage, and employment to one of Kenya's most marginalised regions. Community development agreements funded schools, clinics, and local infrastructure. Ecotrunk's approach mirrors this model: we view community engagement not as a regulatory box to tick but as a foundation for project success and long-term social license to operate.

Grid Integration Challenges

Integrating 310 MW of variable wind power into a grid designed around predictable hydropower and geothermal baseload required sophisticated forecasting, dispatch scheduling, and curtailment protocols. The project invested in a dedicated control centre and worked closely with Kenya Power and KETRACO to ensure grid stability. These integration lessons are directly applicable to Kenya's expanding renewable energy portfolio.

The Role of Small Hydro in Kenya's Rural Electrification

By Ecotrunk Hydropower Team | 6 min read

While much of the attention in Kenya's renewable energy sector focuses on large-scale solar and wind projects, small and mini hydropower plants are quietly transforming lives in rural communities beyond the national grid. These plants, typically ranging from 10 kW to 1 MW, harness the energy of perennial rivers in Kenya's highland regions to provide reliable, 24-hour electricity.

Why Small Hydro Matters

For communities in Kenya's highland regions where perennial rivers flow with reliable gradients, small hydro offers unique advantages. Unlike solar, which stops generating at night, and wind, which varies with weather patterns, hydro can provide consistent baseload power around the clock. A well-designed small hydro plant can operate for 50 years or more with proper maintenance, creating intergenerational value for the communities it serves.

Applications in Rural Development

The impact of reliable electricity on rural communities is transformative. Small hydro plants power schools after dark, enabling adult education programmes. They run grain mills and coffee pulpers, adding value to agricultural produce. They operate cold storage for vaccines at rural health clinics and provide lighting for small businesses to operate beyond daylight hours. In Murang'a and Nandi counties, we have seen small hydro projects catalyse local economic development far beyond electricity generation.

Environmental Considerations

Run-of-river hydro designs, which divert a portion of a river's flow without significant impoundment, offer a low environmental footprint compared to large dam projects. Proper environmental flow management ensures that downstream ecosystems remain healthy, while fish-friendly turbine designs minimise ecological disruption. At Ecotrunk, we advocate for climate-resilient hydropower design that incorporates sediment management and environmental flow releases.

Solar-Plus-Storage: The New Standard for Commercial Energy

By Ecotrunk Energy Storage Team | 7 min read

The convergence of falling solar module prices and dramatic battery cost reductions has created a tipping point for commercial energy in Kenya. Solar-plus-storage systems can now deliver firm, dispatchable power at a lower cost than grid electricity or diesel generation for many commercial and industrial users.

The Battery Cost Revolution

Lithium-ion battery pack prices have fallen by more than 80 percent over the past decade, from over USD 1,000 per kWh in 2015 to under USD 150 per kWh today. At these price levels, adding storage to a solar installation typically increases the system cost by 30 to 50 percent but doubles the useful energy output by capturing generation that would otherwise be curtailed during low-demand periods. The business case is strongest for facilities with evening peak loads or unreliable grid supply.

Technology Options

For commercial applications, lithium-ion batteries remain the dominant technology due to their high energy density, efficiency, and declining cost. Lithium iron phosphate (LFP) chemistry has emerged as the preferred choice for stationary storage due to its safety profile, long cycle life, and lower environmental impact compared to nickel-based chemistries. For larger-scale applications, flow batteries offer advantages in duration and cycle life but at higher upfront cost.

Grid Support Services

Beyond backup power, solar-plus-storage systems can provide valuable grid support services including voltage regulation, frequency response, and peak shaving. As Kenya's grid integrates more variable renewable generation, these services will become increasingly valuable. Forward-thinking commercial clients are positioning themselves to participate in emerging ancillary services markets.

Navigating Kenya's Renewable Energy Regulatory Landscape

By Ecotrunk Regulatory Team | 8 min read

Developing a renewable energy project in Kenya requires navigating a complex regulatory environment involving multiple agencies, permits, and compliance requirements. Understanding this landscape early in project development is essential to avoiding delays and cost overruns.

Key Regulatory Bodies

  • EPRA (Energy and Petroleum Regulatory Authority): Issues generation licenses, approves power purchase agreements, and sets tariff structures. All projects above 1 MW require a generation license.
  • NEMA (National Environment Management Authority): Mandates Environmental and Social Impact Assessment (ESIA) for all energy projects. The ESIA process includes public participation and can take 6 to 12 months.
  • REREC (Rural Electrification and Renewable Energy Corporation): Manages the feed-in tariff programme and supports renewable energy development in rural areas.
  • KPLC (Kenya Power): The off-taker for grid-connected projects. Grid interconnection studies and connection agreements must be negotiated early.
  • KETRACO (Kenya Electricity Transmission Company): Manages the high-voltage transmission grid. Projects connecting to the transmission network require KETRACO approval.
  • County Governments: Increasingly important for land use approvals, business permits, and community engagement protocols.

Practical Tips for Developers

Start regulatory engagement early. Identify required permits during the feasibility phase and begin the ESIA process as soon as a project site is confirmed. Build relationships with regulatory staff and seek pre-application guidance where available. Budget for regulatory timelines of 12 to 18 months from project inception to all permits in hand.

Why Kenya Must Diversify Its Renewable Energy Portfolio

By Ecotrunk Strategy Team | 6 min read

Kenya is already a global leader in renewable energy, with over 90 percent of electricity generated from clean sources. However, the composition of this generation mix presents both opportunities and vulnerabilities that demand strategic attention.

The Current Mix

Geothermal provides approximately 45 percent of Kenya's electricity, hydropower around 35 percent, wind approximately 12 percent, and solar a growing but still modest share. While the high renewable share is commendable, the concentration risk is significant. Drought periods can reduce hydropower output by 40 percent or more, forcing reliance on expensive emergency diesel generation. Geothermal plants, while providing excellent baseload, require significant upfront capital and long development timelines.

The Case for Balance

A balanced renewable energy portfolio distributes risk across multiple technologies with complementary profiles. Solar generates during daylight hours when demand peaks. Wind often blows strongest at night and during cloudy periods, complementing solar. Hydropower with reservoir storage provides dispatchable capacity that can be called upon when needed. Geothermal provides stable baseload regardless of weather conditions. By developing all four technologies in a coordinated manner, Kenya can minimise the impact of any single technology's variability or disruption.

Our Recommendation

Ecotrunk advocates for a deliberate diversification strategy that sets minimum and maximum shares for each technology in the national generation mix. This approach would guide investment decisions, grid planning, and tariff policy towards an optimally balanced portfolio that maximises reliability, minimises cost, and reduces climate risk.
